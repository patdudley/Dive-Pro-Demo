The ocean can be warm and still be starving

San Diego's water-temperature story isn't just "warm" or "cold." The important question is what that warmth does to the conveyor belt carrying food into the coastal ecosystem.

3 September 2026 · Dive Pro

Picture the California coast as a giant elevator. Wind helps pull cold, nutrient-rich water up from depth. That water feeds plankton, which feed small fish, which feed the larger fish, birds, mammals, and basically every animal that makes a morning boat ride worth waking up for.

That elevator is called upwelling. It is the quiet engine underneath the California Current.

Now the engine is running, but the cargo is getting thinner.

NOAA's California Current team says the upwelling amount along the West Coast is currently close to typical for this time of year. But the amount of nitrate being delivered with that upwelled water is below usual levels in some areas. In other words, the elevator is moving. It just isn't carrying the same groceries.

That distinction matters because "upwelling" is not a magic synonym for "good conditions." The water can move vertically without bringing the full nutrient package that normally turns the surface ocean into a buffet.

The bigger backdrop is heat. NOAA says a marine heatwave has continued to affect parts of the coastal region off central and Southern California since 2025. A second heatwave has developed farther offshore. NOAA's forecast gives California waters an 80–100% likelihood of marine heatwave conditions through the end of 2026.

Then there is El Niño, which is developing separately. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center says El Niño is present and has more than a 90% chance of reaching "very strong" levels during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter. The current coastal heatwaves were not directly caused by El Niño, NOAA says. The concern is the handoff: existing heat fades, El Niño-driven warmth arrives, and the ecosystem gets less of a break than the calendar suggests.

This is where ocean science stops being a scoreboard and starts becoming useful.

Warm water does not automatically mean an empty ocean. NOAA's spring California Cooperative Oceanic Fisheries Investigations cruise, which ran between San Diego and San Francisco, found high abundances of sardine and anchovy eggs despite the marine heatwave. Surveys off central California also reported strong young-of-year anchovy and rockfish productivity.

But the same assessment found below-average krill abundance in one survey, with some evidence that krill may have been sitting deeper than usual. That is a very different kind of signal. The ocean can look busy from the surface while the food web underneath has changed floors.

For divers, the practical takeaway is not "warm water is bad." It is that warmth rearranges the menu.

El Niño weakens the normal trade-wind setup in the tropical Pacific, pushing warm water eastward and making coastal upwelling less effective. With fewer nutrients reaching the surface, phytoplankton can decline. That change works upward through zooplankton and forage fish. At the same time, warm water can make Southern California more welcoming to species that usually prefer points south, including yellowtail and albacore tuna.

So the same season can bring tropical visitors and a less reliable pantry. That is the ocean being annoyingly complicated, not contradictory.

Kelp is one of the organisms that pays attention to the pantry. NOAA lists declines in floating and understory kelps among the possible effects of a marine heatwave followed by a strong El Niño. Kelp does not need a dramatic movie-villain event to struggle. A long stretch of warm, nutrient-poor water is enough to make recovery harder, especially when the system has already been stressed by repeated heat events.

None of this is a call to cancel a dive, declare the winter cooked, or turn one index into a prophecy. NOAA's own language is about risk and possible impacts, not a locked script. The useful question is what happens next: Does the heat persist? Does nutrient delivery recover? Do local observations show species shifting depth, place, or timing?

That last question is worth taking seriously. NOAA is asking people on the water to report unusual species, temperatures, depths, catches, and other changes because surveys cannot be everywhere at once. A strange fish, a different layer of life, or a familiar spot that suddenly feels biologically quiet is not a forecast. It is a data point.

The ocean is warm right now. The more revealing story is whether it is still able to feed itself.

Sources: NOAA Integrated Ecosystem Assessment, "California-Current Ocean Assessment Status Takeaways," updated Aug. 26, 2026. NOAA California Current Marine Heatwave Tracker.
