NOAA just turned El Niño up

Monday’s weekly update. Not a winter forecast.

27 August 2026 · Dive Pro

If you were in the water this month and thought, this doesn’t feel like a normal August, NOAA kind of agrees.

On Monday, August 24, NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center dropped its weekly El Niño scoreboard. The main number they watch, Niño-3.4, is +1.8°C. Two other eastern Pacific boxes are even hotter: +2.5°C and +3.2°C. Eleven days ago the official monthly write-up had that main number at +1.4°C. It ticked up. That’s the news. Not “the winter is cooked.” One week of warmer water in the tropical Pacific.

Here’s the thing. NOAA still has an El Niño Advisory. They still say there’s a greater than 90% chance this thing is very strong this fall and winter. For October through December they give a 69% chance it’s historic, meaning stronger than the El Niños since 1950. Then they do the NOAA thing and remind you that even at that size, the usual El Niño impacts are more likely, not guaranteed. I’m taking them at their word. Odds, not a lock.

What that means in La Jolla, in English: we already had a warm lid before this week’s table printed. NOAA Fisheries’ heatwave tracker (updated August 19) is still watching a blob of warm water off central and southern California that’s been hanging around since May 2025. They say most of this summer’s extra heat was not El Niño. They also say the week of August 15 off southern California might finally be some actual El Niño warming sneaking in. Then they say the heatwave and El Niño might not stack on top of each other like two plus two. The risk is just… staying warm. Warm, then still warm.

That’s why this is a blog post and not a forecast recap. Daily vis stays on the homepage. This is the bigger gear behind why the surface has felt like soup, why pelagics have been around, and why the kelp has a harder year when the water won’t cool off. None of that is a promise for Saturday at the Cove. If you want feet, use the forecast. If you want the plot, this is the plot.

I don’t know how else to say it: a strong east-Pacific El Niño usually means a warmer, more layered surface and less of that cold, food-rich water coming up from below. Fish that like the warm stuff can hang. Giant kelp likes the opposite. That’s the shape. It is not “you’ll have 20 feet on Sunday.”

NOAA’s next official write-up is September 10. Until then the Monday table is the live wire. If it moves again, we edit this. We don’t write a sequel.
