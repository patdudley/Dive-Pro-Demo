El Niño tracker / Freediving + spearfishing

El Niño is building. What changes for California divers?

Original charts connect the seasonal Pacific outlook to local water temperature, swell, fish and the dive checks that still matter most.

11 November 2026 · Dive Pro

A warmer Pacific gets your attention when you freedive or spearfish California. Maybe the water feels different. Maybe a species turns up farther north. Maybe a winter storm sends swell toward your usual entry. Those are related questions, but they are not the same forecast.

Source cutoff: November 11, 2026. The CPC discussion available when this story was prepared is dated October 8. It carries an El Niño Advisory and says a strong-to-very strong event is likely through January-March 2027. CPC schedules its next discussion for November 12. The seasonal chart below uses the October outlook, not an unreleased November forecast.

1. The seasonal signal is strong. The local answer is still conditional.

The first chart redraws NOAA's numerical outlook, rather than borrowing someone else's weather graphic. Each line is a forecast range for a three-month tropical Pacific index. It is not a water-temperature forecast for La Jolla, and it is not a probability of a good dive.

For October-December, the forecast median is +2.74°C, with a 5th-95th percentile range of +2.25 to+3.23°C. That is a large seasonal signal. NOAA also warns that a large index does not guarantee a particular impact. A stronger event changes the background odds; it does not schedule the next swell or tell you which cove will be clear.

2. Warm surface water is not the whole dive.

NOAA Fisheries describes two connections to West Coast warming: coastally trapped waves can deepen the thermocline, and atmospheric changes can weaken the winds that drive cold-water upwelling. Their effects differ by place and timing. The same NOAA report warns that coastal marine heatwaves can arrive before El Niño's effects.

So start with a local measurement. The second chart shows near-surface temperature and significant wave height at Scripps Nearshore buoy 46254. These are observations from one buoy, not Dive Pro model estimates.

In this short window, measured surface water ranged from 75.0 to 76.5°F while significant wave height ranged from about 2.3 to 3.6 feet. That is not enough evidence to attribute either line to El Niño. There is no seasonal baseline in this chart, and a near-surface sensor cannot tell you the temperature below a thermocline.

For a freediver, the useful question is what the water is doing at the depths and places you plan to visit. Use a recent local profile or report when one exists. Do not pick exposure protection from an equatorial index or one surface reading.

3. A busier storm track is not a daily swell forecast.

NOAA describes a tendency for the subtropical jet to shift south and extend east during El Niño, favoring stormier conditions in parts of California. Elevated coastal sea level can add to the effects of tides and storms. That is a seasonal tendency, not a promise of larger surf at every California beach every day.

At the dive site, read swell height with period and direction, including the secondary swell. Then consider shoreline exposure, wind, tide and the actual entry. The buoy's significant wave height is not breaking-wave height at the beach. A weather-map arrow offshore is not an underwater-current measurement.

For visibility, the important chain is local. Waves and currents can lift bottom sediment. Particles may remain after the surface calms. Runoff is a separate problem: San Diego County advises avoiding ocean and bay contact for 72 hours after rainfall ends, and current advisories or closures can require staying out longer. Clear-looking water does not establish water quality.

4. Fish can move. That does not mean they will be at your reef.

NOAA Fisheries reports that warm-water migrants such as dorado, striped marlin and yellowfin tuna can extend north in warm conditions, and market squid can shift north too. But marine heatwaves can produce similar movements without El Niño. A sighting is not a clean diagnostic of the climate pattern.

For spearfishers, treat these as reasons to watch verified local observations, not a species forecast. Offshore pelagic distributions do not establish fish availability at a nearshore reef. Habitat, prey, currents and local conditions still matter. Nothing in these charts changes current fishing rules, protected-area boundaries or your own limits.

The ecosystem response is not simply warmer equals better. NOAA's account of 2024 describes delayed upwelling followed by strong productivity, while harmful algal blooms still affected marine life. A single climate label can sit above very different local outcomes.

The dive check stays local.

  • Temperature: distinguish surface readings from dive-depth conditions.
  • Swell: check both components, their periods and directions, plus exposure.
  • Visibility: use recent observations from the same area. Smaller waves are not proof of recovery.
  • Water quality: check rain, advisories and closures separately from clarity.
  • Fish: separate confirmed local sightings from seasonal expectations.
